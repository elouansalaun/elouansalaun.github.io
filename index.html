Public procurement is about 13% of GDP across OECD countries, and in guarding and patrol services almost every contract is won or lost through a call for tenders. Yet the bid itself is usually priced from memory: a margin that worked on a job that felt comparable, corrected by instinct for a competitor someone met last year. The paradox is that the market publishes nearly everything needed to do better, specifications, opening results, awards, amendments, and almost none of it is used.

This is my MSc thesis, carried out at Polytechnique Montréal, in collaboration with a leading provider of security services in Québec. It is one system, not five experiments: a scraper that finds the tender, a retrieval pipeline that reads its PDF, a similarity model that finds what the company already knows about contracts like it, a ranking model that proposes a margin, and an interface a bid manager actually opens. Each module is only as useful as the one feeding it, which is the whole difficulty of building it end to end.

Five modules, one database

The framework is deliberately built around a single store rather than a pipeline of scripts. Modules 1 and 2 write to it, modules 3 and 4 read from it and write back their derived objects, and module 5 only reads. That constraint is what allows a tender scraped on Monday to be scored, compared and priced on Tuesday without anybody exporting a spreadsheet.

02 — Monitoring

Recognising a contract you have already priced once

Tenders are published on SEAO, the Québec government electronic tendering system: specifications, submission forms, contractual clauses, addenda. Open Canada gives the publication URLs, scraping gives the rest — descriptions, bid opening results, awards, amendments. Collected over January 2015 to March 2026, that is 1,960 publications, with award information available for 94% of them: who bid, at what price, and who won.

The useful part is not the list, it is the link. A tender that is a renewal of a contract the company already holds, or already lost, is the single most informative prior available at bid time. Nothing in the data says so explicitly, so the match is made on descriptive attributes with sequence-based entity resolution (SequenceMatcher, Levenshtein distance) across client names, sites and descriptions that are never written the same way twice. Over 2025 the process retrieved 60 tenders with 92% concordance, enough to turn the dashboard into an early-warning list rather than a news feed.

03 — Reading the documents

A retrieval pipeline shaped like the contracts it reads

What actually drives cost in a guarding contract is buried in prose: how many uniforms of which type, what equipment, whether a patrol vehicle is required and who supplies it. These are sentences in a PDF, spread across sections that vary in position from one client to the next.

Because the contracts follow highly regular formats, PyMuPDF can label headers, paragraphs and lists structurally, and that structure is kept: chunks are built hierarchically, each one carrying the section it belongs to instead of being a flat 500-token window. Retrieval then filters by level before scoring, so a query about vehicles is answered from the operational section rather than from a clause that merely mentions the word.

Retrieval is sparse, BM25 over vectors enriched with a security-domain vocabulary, and that is a deliberate choice over dense embeddings. The vocabulary here is narrow, technical and exact; the difference between two uniform types is one adjective, and lexical matching does not blur it. The retrieved chunks go into a prompt carrying explicit extraction rules and a JSON output schema, and Ministral-3-14B-instruct returns the structured record.

From words to a number that can be compared

Extracted entities are matched against a hand-built cost dictionary using spaCy, exactly where possible and partially otherwise, then aggregated per category. A contract stops being a PDF and becomes three numbers: what its uniform, equipment and vehicle requirements are worth. The validation is indirect but convincing, the distribution of uniform scores lands on the price points the company actually charges.

04 — Similarity

Teaching a distance what "comparable" means to a bid manager

Every contract is described by operational, financial and contextual variables: region, client type, duration, price, workload, resource requirements, and the attributes coming out of the RAG pipeline. Continuous variables are min-max scaled, and each variable type gets the comparison it deserves, categorical similarity matrices, sequence comparison on names, Euclidean distance on the numeric block, a temporal decay so a 2016 contract counts less than a 2024 one, Jaccard on sets of requirements. The individual distances are then aggregated into a single score.

The aggregation weights are the only place where judgment enters, so they are the thing to learn. An expert was asked, over 300 iterations, to pick and order the three most comparable contracts out of the top 15 returned by the distance score. Note what is being collected: rankings, not ratings. Experts are unreliable when asked how similar two contracts are on a scale, and very reliable when asked which of two is closer.

A small neural network (3, 2), initialised with equal weights, is then refined on that base with a RankNet pairwise objective, trained on 80% of the expert comparisons and tested on the remaining 20%. It improves both the exact-trio ratio and the mean Spearman rank correlation over the hand-weighted distance, the model ends up agreeing with the expert about ordering, which is what the pricing module downstream consumes.

Why this module carries the whole system

Module 4 never sees a raw contract. It sees the set of past contracts that module 3 declares comparable, and the winning margins attached to them. If the similarity is wrong, the pricing model is confidently answering the wrong question, which is the reason the expert base exists at all, and the reason the evaluation is done on held-out human rankings rather than on the internal loss.

05 — Pricing

Rank the margins, do not regress them

The decision at the end of the chain is one number: the markup ratio to apply. Two things bear on it, the technical characteristics of this contract and the state of the market right now, and the model is built to hold both. Similar contracts come from module 3, their winning markups are re-indexed to today using market evolution indicators derived from rolling means of past winning ratios, and recent tender results are added on top to capture short-term competitive behaviour.

The modelling choice is the interesting part. With only a few exploitable tenders, a regression on the winning margin is both data-hungry and mis-specified: it optimises squared error, while the business loss is violently asymmetric. A margin one point too high loses the contract entirely; a margin one point too low wins it and leaves money on the table. So the problem is reformulated as Learning to Rank: an XGBRanker with a pairwise objective, evaluated with NDCG, orders a grid of candidate markups from 1.05 to 3.00. The target is not a value but a position, the highest candidate that still wins.

What is measured

A bid is not evaluated on its distance to the winning price alone, because a bid that is always close and never first earns nothing. Six metrics are tracked, and they deliberately disagree with each other.

Regression wins the distance metrics, MD 0.250 against 0.280, and loses the money, 1.59 M$ against 3.52 M$. It is not a contradiction, it is the mechanism: to minimise absolute error against an unknown winning margin, the safest move is to shade every bid downwards, which converts wins into thin wins. A second run on 57 tenders, with chronological training sets growing from 31 to 117 contracts, checks that this is not an artefact of one test window: the model ranks first on 33 tenders (59%) and within the top three on 83%, with only two badly underestimated bids.

06 — Interface

The part that decides whether any of it is used

All of the above reaches bid managers through a single Streamlit application with two entry points. The first is monitoring: ongoing tenders, upcoming renewals, and a one-click path from an opportunity into its analysis. The second is the contract view, where technical characteristics can be entered or loaded and the models answer the historical contract this one renews, its outcome and applied margin, the comparable contracts retrieved by module 3, and links back to the original procurement sources so that every number can be traced.

The market overview is the view that changed conversations the most: historical and market-adjusted margins by contract group, and competitor profiling by participation rate, win rate and pricing behaviour. A bid meeting that used to open on "what did we charge last time" now opens on what the market charged, who showed up, and where the margin has drifted since.

Limits

What this does not yet establish

The evaluation is retrospective. Every result above answers "what would this have bid, had it existed", on 54 to 57 exploitable tenders, a small dataset by any standard, and one where a handful of large contracts move the dollar figures. A retrospective win is also a simplification: it assumes competitors would have bid the same way against a different price, which they would not. And the pricing recommendations have not yet been validated prospectively by the experts who would have to sign them.

The natural continuation is a deployment with a feedback loop, so each new opening result retrains the ranker; enrichment of the historical base; extension beyond guarding to other service sectors; and the step the framework makes visible but does not take, optimising a portfolio of simultaneous bids rather than each tender on its own.

Quebec publishes everything about its public procurement. Every call for tenders, every bid amount, every award and every amendment is released as open data in the Open Contracting Data Standard, weekly, since 2020. It is also, as published, close to unusable: multi-gigabyte JSON dumps of deeply nested releases, which is an excellent archive format and a terrible way to answer a question.

And the questions are simple ones. Who holds this buyer's security contract today, and until when. What does guarding actually go for in Montreal. Does this buyer rotate suppliers or renew the same one. How much of the market do the top three firms hold. Each is three joins and one modelling decision away from the raw feed, and it is the modelling decision, never the joins, that quietly returns a plausible wrong number.

So this repository is two things. A three-script pipeline that turns the feed into a query-ready SQLite file, and a read-only MCP server that hands a language model a tool surface over it. The point is not to give a model SQL access, a generic SQLite MCP server would do that in one line. It is to give it the correct SQL, frozen into tools, for the handful of questions where the obvious query is wrong.

01 — Architecture

Three scripts to build, one server to serve

The build side is deliberately three separate scripts writing three separate artifacts, rather than one pipeline object. Each stage is idempotent and can be re-run alone: the fetcher compares the feed's updated timestamp against local file mtimes and downloads only what moved, the filter is a pure function of the dumps on disk, and the normalizer rebuilds the database from scratch every time. Refreshing the data next month is three commands, not a migration.

Nothing in DATA/ is committed. The weekly dumps are several gigabytes and the database is a derived artifact, so the repository ships the recipe and not the result.

What gets kept

The filter is the one place where the scope of the whole project is decided, and it is eight lines at the bottom of build_raw_json_db.py: a release is kept if its title matches one of eight French keywords ( gardiennage, agents de sécurité, service de surveillance, patrouill and so on ) and does not mention incendie, or if its first item carries one of six UNSPSC codes in the public-safety family. Releases are then deduplicated on ocid, since a process reappears in every weekly dump it was touched in. Point those two lists at another sector and the rest of the repository is unchanged.

02 — Schema

OCDS is nested, questions are relational

An OCDS release is one deeply nested object per contracting process: parties, tender, items, lots, bids, awards, contracts, amendments and payment transactions all hanging off a single ocid. Normalizing it is mostly patient work, the source mixes NEQ/neq, totalAmount/totalamount, durationInDay/durationInDays, so every field goes through a pick() helper that takes the first present case variant, but two structural decisions carry the whole design.

The first: party ids are release-scoped. The OP-387 in one process is not the OP-387 in another, so every party-keyed table carries the ocid and every join is on the pair (ocid, party_id). The second: the only identifier that is stable across processes is the NEQ, Quebec's enterprise number, which is why it gets its own index and why every aggregate in the server keys on it.

Two things the database carries for the model, not for me

An FTS5 index built with tokenize = 'unicode61 remove_diacritics 2', aggregating per process the title, the buyer, the supplier names, the item descriptions and every party name. French procurement text is written with and without accents in the same corpus; this makes securite and sécurité the same token, so the model never has to guess the spelling.

And a data dictionary inside the database itself, a schema_doc table of table/column/description rows. The server's describe_schema reads it and hands the model the same notes a new analyst would be given, that value_unit is a SEAO code and not a currency, that municipal is a flag, that value_total_amount is the full-term figure. The documentation travels with the artifact instead of living in a README the model will never read.

03 — Why not a generic SQL server

Three traps that return a plausible wrong number

The tempting answer to all of this is to point an off-the-shelf SQLite MCP server at the file and let the model write its own SQL. It works, in the sense that it always returns a table. Three properties of this data make that table wrong often enough to be worse than useless, and none of the three announce themselves: the query succeeds, the number looks reasonable, and it is off by a factor.

04 — Tool surface

A flexible core, and six answers already worked out

The surface is deliberately hybrid. Six curated tools cover the questions worth getting right, with the modelling baked in and nothing left for the model to decide; three flexible tools cover everything else. describe_schema is the map, search_processes is the usual entry point, and run_sql is the escape hatch for the long tail, the questions I did not anticipate, which are most of them.

What the curated tools return is not rows. It is a short report: amounts formatted in dollars, region codes expanded to names (6 → Montreal), bid unit codes translated to what they mean, percentiles rather than raw distributions, and a closing line stating what was excluded and why. The tool answers in the analyst's vocabulary so the model does not have to reconstruct it from integers.

05 — Guardrails

Read-only by construction, not by instruction

A tool that executes model-written SQL is a tool that executes model-written SQL. Asking politely for SELECT only is not a control, so run_sql is fenced at four independent levels and none of them depends on the model behaving.

  • The connection cannot write. Every call opens file:…/seao.db?mode=ro through the SQLite URI interface. A write is refused by SQLite itself, whatever passed the parser.
  • One statement, one verb. The query must start with SELECT or WITH, may not contain a semicolon, and is matched against a whole-word blocklist of twelve verbs, insert, drop, attach, pragma, vacuum and the rest — so stacked statements have nowhere to go.
  • Bounded results. A LIMIT is appended when the model omits one, and the caller's limit is itself clamped to 2,000 rows. An unbounded scan cannot flood the context window.
  • Bounded time. Each query runs under a threading.Timer that fires conn.interrupt after five seconds. A pathological join is cancelled rather than hanging the client.
  • No shared state. A fresh connection per call, which keeps things thread-safe under FastMCP's worker pool and costs nothing on a database this size.

The same logic runs in reverse for correctness: test_mcp_tools.py calls all nine tools with the README's own example questions against the built database, and includes a DELETE that is expected to be refused. It is a smoke test rather than a suite, but it is the one that catches a broken build before a model has to discover it mid-conversation.

02 — Markowitz

A constrained optimizer, and the reason not to trust it

The whole frontier rests on one scipy.optimize.minimize call with SLSQP. minimize_vol minimizes √(wᵀΣw) under two equality constraints, weights sum to 1, and portfolio return equals a target, with bounds (0, 1) on every weight, which is how the repo forbids short selling. optimal_weights sweeps a grid of target returns between er.min() and er.max() and traces the curve.

Two portfolios on that curve are found without a target return. msr maximizes the Sharpe ratio by minimizing its negative. gmv is the neat trick : msr(0, np.repeat(1, n), cov) feed the optimizer identical expected returns for every asset, and since it can no longer improve the Sharpe ratio through the numerator, it can only lower volatility. The global minimum variance portfolio falls out of the max-Sharpe solver, and it never touches an expected-return estimate.

Estimation error is amplified, not absorbed

Notebook 8 is the demolition. Take two industries whose true annualized returns over the window are almost identical, Food at 11.68%, Steel at 11.58%, and perturb the estimates by a point or two. The optimizer does not spread the error; it goes to a corner.

What the repo concludes

Two escapes are proposed, both consisting of estimating less. The equally weighted portfolio requires no estimation at all and sits well inside the frontier but survives contact with the future. The GMV requires only a covariance matrix, a far more stable object than expected returns. Neither is optimal ex ante; both are defensible ex post.

03 — The pivot

Cash is not the risk-free asset

Measured in absolute value, cash never loses. Measured as a funding ratio, assets over the present value of the liability, it collapses as soon as rates fall, because the liability's present value rises. The genuinely risk-free asset becomes the one whose price moves like the liability: a zero-coupon bond of matching maturity, approximated in practice by a bond portfolio of matching Macaulay duration.

04 — Monte Carlo

Two generators, and a distribution instead of an average

Simulation is what lets the repo test strategies that have no closed form. Two processes feed everything downstream.

GBM (gbm) draws (n_steps, n_scenarios) normal shocks in one numpy call and cumulative-products them into price paths, no Python loop. Returns are drawn already in 1+R form, and the first row is forced to 1 so every path starts at s_0.

CIR (cir) is the interest rate side: dr = a(b − r)dt + σ√r dW. The mean-reversion term pulls rates back to b, and the √r factor shuts volatility down as rates approach zero, which is what keeps them positive. Because the model works in the instantaneous short rate, ann_to_inst / inst_to_ann (log1p / expm1) convert at both ends. The same function also returns the analytic zero-coupon price P = A(t,T)e−B(t,T)r, so one call gives you both the rate path and the price of the hedging asset.

The discretization trap

Notebook 11 spends real effort on one line. Drawing per-step returns centred on mu*dt + 1 looks right and is not: over 10 years at μ = 7%, terminal prices average 200.4 against a theoretical 196.7, a consistent upward bias of nearly 2%. Centring on (1+mu)**dt instead gives 196.2. Compounding is multiplicative; the drift has to be too. It is the kind of error a simulation will never flag on its own.

Why the average is the wrong summary

The repo makes a point of not collapsing 5,000 scenarios into one number. terminal_stats reports the mean and standard deviation of terminal wealth, but also p_breach (probability of finishing below a floor) and e_short (expected shortfall conditional on breaching). You live one path, not the average of five thousand, so the shape of the distribution is the result, and the mean is a footnote.

05 — CPPI

The feedback loop that turns a wish into a guarantee

Constant Proportion Portfolio Insurance is the one genuinely dynamic algorithm in the repo, and everything in notebooks 10 through 18 is a variation on it. At each step: compute the cushion, the fraction of account value sitting above the floor; invest a multiple m of that cushion in the risky asset, clipped to [0, 1]; put the rest in the safe asset; let the market move; repeat. Because the cushion shrinks as you approach the floor, the risky weight goes to zero before the floor is hit, the protection is mechanical, not predictive.

Notebook 10 also makes a performance point in passing: this is one of the rare places where a Python for loop is unavoidable, because each step depends on the previous account value. Everything else in the toolkit vectorizes; this cannot.

The multiplier is the whole risk decision

m sets how aggressively the cushion is levered, and its reciprocal is the tolerance: 1/m is the largest single-period drop the strategy can absorb before the floor is breached. At m = 3 that is a 33% monthly loss, comfortable. At m = 10 it is 10%, and rebalancing monthly is no longer often enough. This is gap risk, and it is visible in the numbers.

The allocator as an argument

The cleanest piece of engineering in the repo is that bt_mix(r1, r2, allocator, **kwargs) knows nothing about the strategy. A gala serving 3,000 attendees can be modelled, from the bar’s perspective, as a continuous high-volume transaction stream. Historically, operations were managed through manual inventory estimation: sales were recorded as tally marks, stock levels were checked intermittently between service periods, and runners were dispatched to the storeroom when a bottle rack appeared to be approaching depletion. While this process is sufficient to complete service, it provides no real-time visibility into inventory consumption, which bar is approaching a stockout, or the estimated time remaining before replenishment is required.

Each point of sale is equipped with a physical input device that records individual product transactions. Transaction data is consolidated into a shared operational spreadsheet accessible to the organizing team, while a lightweight forecasting layer converts the incoming consumption stream into replenishment recommendations. The objective is to identify potential stockouts approximately 30 minutes in advance, providing sufficient lead time for inventory movement and restocking.

01 — Architecture

From a button press to a row, and back to the storeroom

The chain is deliberately short and one-directional. A press is timestamped and published on the spot; a gateway carries it out of the building over 4G; a broker fans it out; a bridge appends it to a Google Sheet, which is the only place the whole team looks. The loop closes on the other side: the forecast reads the same sheet and produces the one output that matters on the floor, which bar to refill next.

02 — The device

A grid of buttons is the whole interface

Each point of sale gets an ESP32 and a matrix grid of buttons, one per product. We use a matrix of buttons to allow for a compact interface that can accommodate multiple products without requiring a large number of individual pins outputs.

A press becomes a message immediately: device id, monotonic sequence number, product index, and a timestamp taken from the device clock synchronised at boot. The sequence number is what makes the whole chain safe to retry, since a duplicated message is dropped downstream instead of inflating the evening's sales. A long-press is interpreted as a request to cancel the last press.

03 — Getting the data out

The venue's network is not an option, so it is not used

The system uses a router providing a private access point the bars associate with, and a 4G uplink out of the building.

On top of that link, MQTT rather than HTTP, for failures reasons. Messages are tiny and frequent, the broker absorbs a reconnection without the publisher knowing.

Why the queue matters more than the protocol

A 4G link in a basement drops. The decision that made the evening reliable was not the choice of MQTT but the rule that a press is acknowledged locally and published asynchronously: the operator's loop and the network's loop are separated.

04 — Centralisation

A spreadsheet, on purpose

The bridge subscribes to the sale topics and appends one row per message to a Google Sheet: timestamp, bar, product, quantity, sequence. Deduplication happens here, keyed on (device, seq), which is what allows the device to republish freely after a reconnection.

Every person who needs the numbers, has the Google Sheet on their phone, with no account to create and no dashboard to learn. Pivots per bar and per product are built once, before the evening; from then on the sheet updates itself and the team reads a view they already recognise. It is also the archive: the night ends with a complete, per-second record of what was consumed where, which is precisely what next year's forecast is fitted on.

05 — Forecasting the shortage

Three priors, one live counter, one decision

Knowing what has been sold is not the goal, it is the input. What the storeroom needs is the answer to : if nothing changes, when do I run out?

The expected consumption rate of a point of sale is built as a base rate corrected by two multipliers, then continuously re-anchored on what the sheet is actually recording tonight:

$$\lambda_b(t)\;=\;\underbrace{\bar c_b}_{\text{last year, same bar}}\;\times\;\underbrace{m(t)}_{\text{moment of the night}}\;\times\;\underbrace{w_b}_{\text{location}}$$

and the alert is the comparison of the projected time to stock-out with the runner's round trip L:

$$\tau_b(t)\;=\;\min\Big\{\Delta>0\;:\;S_b(t)-\int_t^{t+\Delta}\lambda_b(u)\,\mathrm{d}u\;\le\;0\Big\}\;,\qquad \text{restock } b \text{ when } \tau_b(t)\le L$$
prior
Last year, same bar

Consumption from the previous editions, resampled onto a fifteen-minute grid, gives the shape of the night rather than a single average: a gala does not drink uniformly.

programme

Welcome drinks, seated dinner, speeches, the DJ set, the last hour. Each segment is a multiplier: the bars are nearly idle during dinner and take their whole evening's volume in the two hours after it.

Distance to the dance floor, to the entrance and to the smoking area, and how many other points of sale are visible from the same spot. Two identical bars twenty metres apart do not sell the same thing at the same speed.

The nose cone of the ASTREOS demonstrator is wound from flax fibre in an Elium® thermoplastic matrix on a four-axis XWinder machine. Winding is the natural process for the shape, but it comes with a question that has to be answered before the machine is switched on: what path does the fibre follow on the mandrel, and what does the machine have to do to put it there? A fibre laid along the wrong curve slides down the cone before the resin cures, which is exactly what the first experimental trials showed at the small end.

The path is not a free choice. On a surface of revolution it is the solution of a three-state ordinary differential equation built from the two fundamental forms of the surface and a single dimensionless friction parameter. This résumé is the mathematical and computational half of the project: the derivation of that system, the numerical scheme that solves it, the algebra that turns one fibre path into a complete layer, and the inverse kinematics and time law that turn that layer into a machine programme.

01 — The chain

From a profile on a drawing to a block of G-code

Two solvers were written. The first, integrates a single out-and-back pass and replicates it n times with a rigid rotation; it is cheap and it produced the parametric studies below. The second, integrates the whole winding as one uninterrupted state and converts it into four time-stamped machine axes. The mandrel, the ODE and the Runge–Kutta stepper are shared: version 2 imports them rather than duplicating them.

The important structural point is that everything downstream of the ODE is either an integration of it, a boundary condition on it, or a map applied to its output. There is one model, and it is fifteen lines long.

02 — Geometry

The mandrel is a surface of revolution

The mandrel is generated by a profile \(r(z)>0\) turned about the axis:

$$P(z,\theta)\;=\;\big(\,r(z)\cos\theta,\;r(z)\sin\theta,\;z\,\big),\qquad g(z)\;=\;\sqrt{1+r'(z)^2}$$

\(g\) is the meridian metric factor: a step \(\mathrm{d}z\) along the profile is \(g\,\mathrm{d}z\) of arc on the surface. The coordinate fields \(P_z\) and \(P_\theta\) are orthogonal, so the first fundamental form is diagonal,

$$\mathrm{d}s^2\;=\;g^2\,\mathrm{d}z^2\;+\;r^2\,\mathrm{d}\theta^2$$

and because \(F=M=0\) the meridians and parallels are the lines of curvature. The principal curvatures fall out of the second fundamental form without any algebra:

$$\kappa_1\;=\;-\frac{r''}{g^{3}}\quad\text{(meridian)},\qquad \kappa_2\;=\;\frac{1}{r\,g}\quad\text{(parallel)},\qquad K\;=\;\kappa_1\kappa_2\;=\;-\frac{r''}{r\,g^{4}}$$

On this profile \(r''=-3.6\times10^{-3}\lt 0\) everywhere, so \(\kappa_1,\kappa_2>0\) and \(K>0\): the mandrel is convex, and a fibre laid on it presses onto it in every direction. On a concave meridian the fibre can lift off the surface and bridge, and the whole contact argument of section 03 has to be re-examined. It does not happen here.

The winding angle \(\alpha\) is measured in the tangent plane from the meridian towards the parallel, and it is kept signed and unwrapped. Values with \(|\alpha|>90^\circ\) simply mean the fibre is travelling towards \(-z\). That single convention is what later allows the outbound leg, the turnaround and the return leg to be one continuous solution of one system, with no case distinction.

03 — Mechanics

One dimensionless number decides whether the fibre stays put

Take an element of impregnated tow of length \(\mathrm{d}s\) under tension \(F\). Its curvature vector splits in the Darboux frame into a component in the tangent plane and a component along the normal,

$$\kappa\vec\beta\;=\;\kappa_g\,\hat v\;+\;\kappa_n\,\hat N ,\qquad \vec F_v\;=\;F\kappa_g\,\hat v\,\mathrm{d}s\;\;\text{(slides it)},\qquad \vec F_k\;=\;F\kappa_n\,\hat N\,\mathrm{d}s\;\;\text{(presses it down)}$$

The surface reaction can only push, so the element stays in contact only while \(\kappa_n>0\), guaranteed here by the convexity established above. The available friction is \(|\vec F_f|\le\mu|\vec F_n|=\mu F\kappa_n\,\mathrm{d}s\), so the tangential force can be balanced only if \(F|\kappa_g|\,\mathrm{d}s\le\mu F\kappa_n\,\mathrm{d}s\). The tension cancels, and what is left is a purely geometric criterion:

$$\lambda\;=\;\frac{\kappa_g}{\kappa_n}\;,\qquad\qquad \text{no slip}\iff|\lambda|\;\le\;\mu$$

Two cases organise everything that follows. \(\lambda=0\) means \(\kappa_g=0\): the path is a geodesic, it requires no friction at all and cannot slip. \(\lambda\neq0\) is a non-geodesic path held in place by friction; it buys the freedom to put the turnaround where the design wants it rather than where the geometry puts it, at the price of a friction margin that has to be verified.

In the code \(\lambda\) is a prescribed parameter, not a computed one, the standard modelling choice in the dry-winding literature. The consequence is worth stating plainly: the solver can produce a path that no real friction coefficient would hold, so \(|\lambda|\) is a design output to be reported and checked against a measured \(\mu\). The slippage seen experimentally at the small end, where the trials used a constant 45°, is consistent with the demanded \(|\lambda|\) having exceeded the available \(\mu\) there.

04 — The trajectory equations

Euler for the normal curvature, Liouville for the geodesic one

The normal curvature in the fibre direction is given directly by Euler's theorem, \(\kappa_n=\kappa_1\cos^2\alpha+\kappa_2\sin^2\alpha\), which with the principal curvatures of section 02 becomes

$$\kappa_n\;=\;\frac{1}{g}\left[\,\frac{\sin^2\alpha}{r}\;-\;\frac{r''\cos^2\alpha}{g^{2}}\,\right]$$

The geodesic curvature comes from Liouville's formula for an orthogonal parametrisation. On a surface of revolution the meridians are geodesics, so one of the two coordinate terms vanishes and the parallels contribute \(-r'/(rg)\):

$$\kappa_g\;=\;\frac{\mathrm{d}\alpha}{\mathrm{d}s}\;-\;\frac{r'\sin\alpha}{r\,g}$$

Substituting \(\kappa_g=\lambda\kappa_n\) and adding the two kinematic relations read off the metric gives the model — an autonomous three-state system in \(y=(z,\theta,\alpha)\), parametrised by arc length:

$$\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{\mathrm{d}s}=\frac{\cos\alpha}{g},\qquad \frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{\mathrm{d}s}=\frac{\sin\alpha}{r},\qquad \frac{\mathrm{d}\alpha}{\mathrm{d}s}=\lambda\,\kappa_n\;-\;\frac{r'\sin\alpha}{r\,g}$$

That is the whole physics of the project, and in the code it is fifteen lines:

def winding_rhs(state, mandrel, lam):
    z, _theta, a = state
    r, rp, rpp = mandrel.r(z), mandrel.dr(z), mandrel.d2r(z)
    sa, ca = math.sin(a), math.cos(a)
    g  = math.sqrt(1.0 + rp * rp)                # meridian metric factor
    kn = (sa * sa / r - rpp * ca * ca / (g * g)) / g
    kg = lam * kn                                # lambda = kg / kn
    dz     = ca / g
    dtheta = sa / r
    dalpha = kg - rp * sa / (r * g)
    return np.array([dz, dtheta, dalpha])

Two things about this right-hand side matter more than they look. First, \(\theta\) does not appear in it: the system is invariant under rotation about the axis. That symmetry is what makes the entire pattern algebra of section 09 possible, because a circuit can be shifted in \(\theta\) without re-integrating it.

Second, the choice of parameter. Dividing the third equation by the first recovers exactly the form used in the original project report,

$$\frac{\mathrm{d}\alpha}{\mathrm{d}z}\;=\;\frac{g}{\cos\alpha}\left(\lambda\kappa_n-\frac{r'\sin\alpha}{rg}\right)\;=\;\lambda\left[\frac{\sin\alpha\tan\alpha}{r}-\frac{r''\cos\alpha}{1+r'^2}\right]-\frac{r'}{r}\tan\alpha$$

The two are algebraically identical, the symbolic residual is exactly zero, but they do not behave the same way. The \(\mathrm{d}\alpha/\mathrm{d}z\) form contains \(\tan\alpha\), which diverges as \(\alpha\to90^\circ\); equivalently, \(z\) stops being a valid parameter for the curve at the very point where the curve becomes tangent to a parallel. Any integrator marching in \(z\) has to stop before the turnaround and restart afterwards with a separate model of the reversal.

The arc-length system has no such singularity. \(\cos\alpha\) simply changes sign, \(\mathrm{d}z/\mathrm{d}s\) goes negative, and the integration continues. A complete out-and-back pass comes out of a single integration. That is the change that makes the continuous solver possible at all.

05 — Clairaut

A conserved quantity, a hard design limit, and a free error estimator

For \(\lambda=0\) the system has a first integral, obtained in one line:

$$\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}z}\big(r\sin\alpha\big)=r'\sin\alpha+r\cos\alpha\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\alpha}{\mathrm{d}z}=r'\sin\alpha-r'\sin\alpha=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad r\sin\alpha\;=\;r_0\sin\alpha_0$$

Clairaut's theorem, and three consequences the whole study leans on.

The polar opening

The turnaround happens where \(\alpha=90^\circ\), so the smallest radius a geodesic launched at \((r_0,\alpha_0)\) can reach is \(r_p=r_0\sin\alpha_0\). At \(\alpha_0=25^\circ\) that is 26.271 mm. The solver, integrating the ODE with no knowledge whatsoever of the closed form, returns 26.2708 mm.

A hard limit on the initial angle

A geodesic reaches the tip only if \(r_p\le r_t\), i.e.

$$\alpha_0\;\le\;\alpha_0^{\max}\;=\;\arcsin\frac{r_t}{r_0}\;=\;\arcsin\frac{12.270}{62.162}\;=\;11.384^\circ$$

This is the quantitative version of the observation in the original report that "beyond \(\alpha_i=40^\circ\) the angle becomes too high before the end of the trajectory". In fact the effect starts at 11.4°, and by 40° the geodesic turns around at \(r_p=39.96\) mm, barely two thirds of the way up the cone. A geodesic layer at a usable angle necessarily leaves a polar opening, which is consistent with closing the nose with a separate machined part.

A free error estimator

Since \(r\sin\alpha\) is exactly conserved by the true flow, its drift along a numerical solution is an a posteriori measure of the integration error that costs nothing and needs no reference solution. It is printed on every geodesic run. Its one limitation is worth stating: it only tests the geodesic case, for \(\lambda\neq0\) the quantity is supposed to drift, and the drift then measures the friction being used, not the error.

06 — Friction as a design variable

Choosing λ is choosing where the fibre turns around

For \(\lambda\neq0\) no first integral is available and the ODE has to be integrated. The sign convention determines how the parameter is used, so it is worth stating plainly: with \(\kappa_n>0\) on this mandrel, \(\lambda>0\) adds a positive \(\mathrm{d}\alpha/\mathrm{d}s\) and steers the fibre towards the hoop, so the turnaround happens earlier, at a larger radius; \(\lambda\lt 0\) steers it towards the meridian and pushes the turnaround further up the cone.

07 — Numerics

The integrator is not what limits the accuracy

Both solvers advance the system with classical explicit fourth-order Runge–Kutta. The scheme is kept deliberately: the right-hand side is smooth wherever the spline is \(C^2\), the problem is non-stiff, and a fixed step in arc length is exactly what the downstream band and coverage calculations want, because it samples the fibre uniformly in physical length rather than in \(z\). The step has a direct physical reading, \(\Delta s=0.5\) mm is half a millimetre of tow per step, about 830 steps for one pass on this mandrel.

Where the accuracy actually goes

The turnaround is not a fixed time but a state event: the leg ends when \(\cos\alpha=0\), or when the carriage limit is reached. How that event is located turns out to dominate the accuracy of the whole computation.

Version 1 takes a full RK4 step and then tests the sign, so the turnaround is recorded at the first sample past 90°, with an error of order \(\Delta s\), and non-monotone, because the sign of the overshoot depends on where the last step happens to land. Version 2 treats an event as a sign change of a scalar guard \(g(y)\) and refines it by bisecting the step itself:

if hit >= 0:
    lo, hi, gfun = 0.0, ds, events[hit][1]
    for _ in range(60):                       # bisect the RK4 step
        mid = 0.5 * (lo + hi)
        if gfun(_rk4_step(st, mid, mandrel, lam)) < 0.0:
            lo = mid
        else:
            hi = mid
    st = _rk4_step(st, hi, mandrel, lam)          # land exactly on the event

The cost is 60 extra right-hand-side evaluations per event, about 0.3 % of a leg. The benefit is that the leg lands on \(\alpha=90^\circ\) to \(1.4\times10^{-14}\) degrees at every step size tested, and the turnaround radius then converges to Clairaut's value at the full fourth order of the integrator: \(1.3\times10^{-7}\) mm at \(\Delta s=2\) mm falling to \(3.1\times10^{-11}\) mm at 0.25 mm. Because each leg starts exactly on the event, the error does not accumulate from circuit to circuit.

08 — The turnaround, as a curve

Prescribe the angle, derive the path

The first solver reverses the fibre with the mirror map \(\alpha\mapsto\pi-\alpha\), which preserves \(|\sin\alpha|\), so \(\theta\) keeps advancing in the same sense, while flipping \(\cos\alpha\), so the carriage reverses. On paper that is a clean idealisation. On a machine it is not realisable: the delivery eye would have to yaw through 130° in zero time. The extra rotation the pattern needs is likewise spliced in as a synthetic arc in which \(z\), \(\theta\) and \(\alpha\) are written down independently rather than being related by the surface kinematics.

The second solver makes the turnaround a genuine piece of the trajectory. The trick is to stop treating \(\alpha\) as a state and start treating it as a prescribed function of a normalised parameter \(\sigma\in[0,1]\), then integrate the two kinematic equations, which remain exact, along with it. For a piece of arc length \(L\):

$$\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{\mathrm{d}\sigma}=L\,\frac{\cos\alpha(\sigma)}{g(z)},\qquad \frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{\mathrm{d}\sigma}=L\,\frac{\sin\alpha(\sigma)}{r(z)}$$

Prescribing \(\alpha\) and deriving \((z,\theta)\) from it gives a path that lies on the surface and whose tangent really does make the angle \(\alpha\) at every point. The swing itself is built from a smoothstep ramp

$$\rho(x)=\operatorname{sgn}(x)\,u^2(3-2u),\quad u=\min(|x|,1),\qquad \text{so } \rho(0)=0,\;\rho(\pm1)=\pm1,\;\rho'(\pm1)=0$$

which is \(C^1\) at both ends, so the angle joins the incoming and outgoing legs with matching slope and there is no kink in the tow direction. A linear ramp would be \(C^0\) only, and the yaw axis would see a step in velocity. Writing \(\beta=\alpha_{\text{in}}-\pi/2\) for the signed half-amplitude, the turnaround is three pieces: ramp in, \(\alpha=\pi/2+\beta\rho(1-\sigma)\); plateau, \(\alpha\equiv\pi/2\), a true hoop at constant \(z\); ramp out, \(\alpha=\pi/2-\beta\rho(\sigma)\).

The dwell is a hoop, and costs no integration

On the plateau \(\alpha=90^\circ\) exactly, so \(\mathrm{d}z/\mathrm{d}s=0\), the carriage is frozen, and \(\mathrm{d}\theta/\mathrm{d}s=1/r\) with \(r\) constant. The arc needed for a prescribed extra rotation \(\delta\) is therefore available in closed form, \(L_{\text{plateau}}=\delta\,r(z_{\text{plateau}})\), and needs no integration at all. Physically it is the wrap that anchors the reversal, not a bookkeeping rotation.

The carriage comes back exactly where it left

This is the property that makes circuits stack, and it is provable rather than lucky. By the kinematics \(\mathrm{d}z\propto\cos\alpha\). On the ramp in, \(\cos\alpha=\cos(\pi/2+\beta\rho(1-\sigma))=-\sin(\beta\rho(1-\sigma))\); on the ramp out, \(\cos\alpha=+\sin(\beta\rho(\sigma))\). Under \(\sigma\mapsto1-\sigma\) the two integrands are exact negatives, and the plateau contributes nothing because \(\cos90^\circ=0\). The net axial displacement is zero. Measured on every run, the residual, which comes only from the \(z\)-dependence of \(g\) and \(r\), is \(2.5\times10^{-12}\) mm.

What does not cancel is the excursion during the turn. The carriage travels past its entry station and comes back, an overtravel proportional to the ramp arc. With the default 15 mm ramp the base turnaround runs 5.37 mm past \(z=0\), off the end of the fitted profile, which is not an artefact to be suppressed but a statement about the tooling: that is where the turnaround ring or pin band has to provide material, and it is why the surface is extended as a cylinder beyond the profile.

One subtlety in sizing the dwell: the ramps themselves sweep \(\theta\), because a fibre at \(\alpha\) near 90° wraps the mandrel fast. That sweep belongs to the free motion, not to the dwell. The solver therefore runs a dwell-free probe circuit first and measures the free sweep, \(\Delta\theta_f=\Delta\theta_{\text{out}}+\Delta\theta_{\text{tip ramp}}+\Delta\theta_{\text{back}}+\Delta\theta_{\text{base ramp}}\), before any pattern arithmetic. On the reference case that is 389.08° per circuit, of which 15.58° comes from the base ramps and exactly 0° from the tip ramps, at a natural turnaround the fibre arrives already at 90° and there is nothing to ramp.

Because every circuit is then integrated from the state the previous one actually left, circuit-to-circuit repeatability becomes a measurement rather than an assumption. Over twelve circuits the closure is \(+6.6\times10^{-4}\) degrees in \(\alpha\) and \(-1.5\times10^{-5}\) mm in \(z\); the rotational invariance of the right-hand side is confirmed instead of being taken on faith. The same log would immediately reveal a drift if a radial offset from previous plies, or a station-dependent \(\lambda\), broke that invariance.

09 — Pattern closure

A ceiling function decides how much fibre a layer costs

For the bands to distribute evenly over \(n_c\) circuits, circuit \(i\) must start at azimuth \(2\pi i p/n_c \pmod{2\pi}\) for some integer \(p\), the pattern number, so the advance per circuit must satisfy

$$\Delta\theta\;=\;2\pi\left(m+\frac{p}{n_c}\right),\quad m\in\mathbb{N},\qquad\text{with } \gcd(p,n_c)=1$$

The coprimality condition is not decorative: if \(\gcd(p,n_c)=d>1\) the circuits visit only \(n_c/d\) distinct positions, each \(d\) times, and the layer has \(d\)-fold gaps. The dwell can only add rotation, never remove it, so \(\Delta\theta\ge\Delta\theta_f\) and the code picks the smallest admissible advance, \(m=\lceil\Delta\theta_f/2\pi-p/n_c\rceil\).

That ceiling is a step, and the step is expensive. Write \(\phi\) for the fractional part of the free sweep in turns. Then \(m\) stays at its smallest value, the dwell stays small, precisely when \(p/n_c>\phi\), that is

$$\phi\;=\;\left\{\frac{\Delta\theta_f}{2\pi}\right\}\;=\;0.0807,\qquad\qquad \boxed{\,n_c\;\lt\;\frac{p}{\phi}\,}$$

As soon as \(n_c\) exceeds \(p/\phi\), the ceiling steps up by one, the advance jumps by a full turn, and the dwell jumps by almost 360° per circuit. That rotation is laid as hoop fibre at the turnarounds, so the fibre consumed by a layer nearly doubles for one extra circuit. For this mandrel \(p/\phi=12.38\) at \(p=1\): twelve circuits cost 5.28 m and thirteen cost 9.31 m.

The criterion also contains its own remedy. The bound scales with \(p\), so a larger pattern number restores the minimal advance at a larger circuit count, subject to coprimality: \(n_c^{\max}(p)=\lfloor p/\phi\rfloor\), giving 12, 24, 37, 61, 86 for \(p=1,2,3,5,7\). The recipe is then one line — choose the number of circuits the coverage requires, then choose the smallest \(p\) with \(n_c\lt p/\phi\) and \(\gcd(p,n_c)=1\).

10 — Coverage

The tow is a band, and the band grows by a factor of eleven

The fibre is not a curve but a band of width \(w\) measured perpendicular to it. Where a band crosses the parallel at station \(z\), the chord it cuts out of that parallel is longer than \(w\), because the band is oblique to the parallel by \(90^\circ-\alpha\). Dividing by the radius gives the angular footprint of one crossing:

$$\Delta\theta_{\text{band}}(z)\;=\;\frac{w}{r(z)\,|\cos\alpha(z)|}\;,\qquad\qquad \Phi(z)\;=\;\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{k\,:\,z(s_k)=z}\frac{w}{r(z)\,|\cos\alpha_k|}$$

With \(w=6\) mm this is 6.10° at the base (\(\alpha=25^\circ\), \(r=62.2\) mm) and 63° at \(z=146\) mm (\(\alpha=78^\circ\), \(r=26.8\) mm): a factor of eleven, of which 2.3 comes from the shrinking radius and 4.8 from the steepening angle. The coverage ratio \(\Phi\) sums that footprint over every crossing of a station, with \(\Phi=1\) meaning exactly one closed layer.

The two codes compute it differently, and the difference is instructive. Version 1 assumes each circuit crosses each station exactly twice, true strictly between the base and the polar opening, and evaluates the closed form \(2n_cw/(2\pi r|\cos\alpha|)\). Version 2 assumes nothing: it scans the whole trajectory for sign changes of \(z(s)-z_{\text{station}}\), interpolates \(\alpha\) at each crossing and sums, so turnarounds, overtravel and hoop plateaus are counted exactly as they were integrated, and the crossing count itself becomes an output. Over the region where the closed form is valid the two agree to a mean of 0.006 % and a worst case of 1.8 %; they diverge precisely where the assumption fails.

Both contain \(1/|\cos\alpha|\), which diverges at the turnaround. That divergence is not a modelling error, the fibre genuinely piles up at the polar opening, and the experimental trials observed exactly that accumulation at the small end, but the band-width formula stops being quantitative there, because the bands overlap rather than tile. Both codes therefore mask stations above 80° and report statistics only over the valid region. Any coverage figure quoted without that mask is meaningless.

The number that follows is the one that should drive the design. Twelve circuits close 41 % of a layer, so \(n_c^{\text{full}}=\lceil n_c/\Phi_{\min}\rceil=\lceil 12/0.406\rceil=30\) circuits are needed for \(\Phi\ge1\) everywhere in the valid region. Section 09 then says how to choose \(p\) so that 30 circuits do not fall over the closure discontinuity: \(p>30\phi=2.42\) requires \(p\ge3\), and coprimality with 30 rejects 3, 4, 5 and 6 and selects \(p=7\). Running the solver at \(n_c=30,\,p=7\) — a pattern it has never seen — returns \(\Phi_{\min}=1.016\), which is the one genuinely predictive check in the whole study.

The ratio of 11 is a property of the trajectory, not of the number of circuits: it is \(\Phi_{\max}/\Phi_{\min}\) within one layer, fixed by the footprint formula and hence by \(\alpha_0\) and the profile. 11 — Four-axis inverse kinematics

The machine follows the eye, not the contact point

The XWinder 4X-23 has four coordinated axes: A, the mandrel spindle, cumulative in degrees; Z, the carriage along the axis; Y, the cross-feed carrying the delivery eye radially; and B, the eye's yaw. The constraint that makes the kinematics non-trivial is that the eye is confined to one axial plane: it cannot move around the mandrel, so the mandrel has to rotate to bring the required azimuth under it.

The tow does not leave the eye at the contact point. It leaves at a point standing off the surface by \(h\) along the normal and leading the contact point by \(\ell\) along the fibre:

$$\mathbf{E}\;=\;\mathbf{C}+h\,\hat n+\ell\,\hat T\quad\Longrightarrow\quad E_r=r+\frac{h}{g}+\frac{\ell r'\cos\alpha}{g},\quad E_\varphi=\ell\sin\alpha,\quad E_z=z-\frac{h r'}{g}+\frac{\ell\cos\alpha}{g}$$

The eye sits in the plane \(\varphi=0\) of the machine frame, so the contact point's azimuth there is \(\theta+A\) and the eye's is \(\theta+\Delta\varphi+A\), which must vanish. With \(\Delta\varphi=\arctan(E_\varphi/E_r)\) and \(r_E=\sqrt{E_r^2+E_\varphi^2}\):

$$A\;=\;-\big(\theta+\Delta\varphi\big),\qquad Z\;=\;E_z+Z_{\text{offset}},\qquad Y\;=\;r_E+Y_{\text{offset}}$$

So \(A\neq-\theta\) and \(Z\neq z\). The error made by ignoring the lead is \(\Delta\varphi\), which reaches 20.9° for \(\ell=20\) mm on this mandrel, 5.8 % of a revolution, far more than any positioning tolerance. \(\theta\) is kept unwrapped throughout, so \(A\) is a continuous cumulative angle running to −4687° (13.0 turns) over the reference programme, which is what the machine's absolute A word expects.

The yaw axis and the taper correction

The eye must line up with the tow, and its yaw axis is radial, so the relevant angle is the tow direction projected into the \((z,\varphi)\) plane, not the surface tangent plane. Taking components of \(\hat T\) in the cylindrical frame, \(\hat T\cdot\hat e_\theta=\sin\alpha\) and \(\hat T\cdot\hat e_z=\cos\alpha/g\), so

$$\tan B\;=\;\sqrt{1+r'^2}\,\tan\alpha\;=\;g\tan\alpha$$

On a cylinder \(g=1\) and \(B=\alpha\); on a taper \(B>\alpha\). It is a small but real correction, 0.005° at the base, 2.62° at \(z=147\) mm for \(\alpha_0=25^\circ\) , and getting it wrong tilts the eye relative to the tow, which is one mechanism by which tow twists on the roller. The trials reported exactly that symptom.

One last subtlety. The tow direction runs continuously from \(\alpha_0\) through 90° to \(180^\circ-\alpha_0\) at every turnaround, which would take \(B\) outside its ±95° travel. But the eye only has to align with the tow's line, defined modulo 180°, so the axis command is the wrapped value, \(B=B_{\text{tow}}-180^\circ\) when \(B_{\text{tow}}>90^\circ\). The wrap happens exactly at the hoop plateau, where +90° and −90° are the same physical orientation, so nothing moves when the label flips. The code deliberately keeps the continuous \(B_{\text{tow}}\) for the velocity computation, which must not see a 180° step, while emitting the wrapped \(B\) as the axis command. Confusing the two is an easy way to write a programme that either exceeds the yaw travel or demands an infinite yaw rate.

12 — Time law

A path is not a trajectory until it has a clock

The inverse kinematics give positions along the fibre. A machine needs positions as functions of time, respecting the axis speed and acceleration limits. Between consecutive path points the four axes must move by \(\Delta A,\Delta Z,\Delta Y,\Delta B_{\text{tow}}\) and the fibre must pay out \(\Delta s\); each imposes a minimum duration and the slowest wins:

$$\Delta t_i=\max_{q\in\{A,Z,Y,B,s\}}\frac{|\Delta q_i|}{\dot q^{\max}},\qquad v_i^{\text{seg}}=\min\!\left(\frac{\Delta s_i}{\Delta t_i},\,v^{\max}_{\text{payout}}\right)$$

The argmax is recorded per segment: it is the limiting axis, and its distribution is the honest way to answer "what is this machine actually waiting for". Speeds are then transferred from segments to points, a point belongs to two segments and cannot exceed either, with \(v_0=v_N=0\) so the run starts and ends at rest, and the tangential acceleration limit is imposed by the two-pass look-ahead used in CNC interpolators:

$$v_{i+1}\leftarrow\min\!\left(v_{i+1},\sqrt{v_i^2+2a^{\max}\Delta s_i}\right),\qquad v_i\leftarrow\min\!\left(v_i,\sqrt{v_{i+1}^2+2a^{\max}\Delta s_i}\right)$$

Working with point speeds is what makes these passes exactly the constant-acceleration relation \(v_2^2=v_1^2+2a\Delta s\) rather than an approximation of it. And under constant acceleration across a segment the mean speed is the arithmetic mean of the endpoint speeds, so \(\Delta t_i=2\Delta s_i/(v_i+v_{i+1})\) is not a trapezoidal approximation but the exact duration for the velocity profile the two passes construct.

13 — G-code

10 597 points, 1 095 blocks, and the same path

The solver runs at \(\Delta s=0.5\) mm. A controller neither wants nor needs 10 597 motion blocks: a helical leg is very nearly a straight line in the four axis coordinates, and the controller interpolates linearly between blocks anyway. The decimator therefore extends a block for as long as every axis stays within tolerance of the straight line the controller would interpolate between its endpoints, using arc length as the interpolation parameter, which is what a constant-feed block actually does:

$$\max_{i\lt k\lt j}\;\left|\,q(s_k)-\left[q(s_i)+\frac{s_k-s_i}{s_j-s_i}\big(q(s_j)-q(s_i)\big)\right]\right|\;\le\;\varepsilon_q$$

The endpoint \(j\) is found by exponential doubling followed by binary search, so the longest admissible block costs \(O(\log n)\) tests rather than \(O(n)\):

step = 1
while i + 2 * step < n and fits(i, i + 2 * step):
    step *= 2                              # exponential search for the bracket
lo, hi = i + step, min(n - 1, i + 2 * step)
while lo < hi:                             # binary search inside it
    mid = (lo + hi + 1) // 2
    if fits(i, mid): lo = mid
    else:            hi = mid - 1

The result is adaptive by construction: the helical legs collapse to a handful of blocks while the turnarounds, where all four axes move fast and non-linearly, keep almost their full resolution. At a 0.05 mm chord tolerance the 10 597 points become 1 095 blocks, a factor of 9.7; at 0.5 mm, 339 blocks, a factor of 31.

Each surviving block is emitted in the X-Winder .X4G dialect, in inches with a decimal comma, preceded by the initialisation preamble (units, absolute positioning, acceleration settings, homing to the limit switches, the move to the first winding point, and M601 to mark the mandrel reference angle \(A=0\)):

G1 F<feed> Z<carriage> E<yaw increment> Y<cross-feed> S<rpm> C<mandrel increment> A<mandrel absolute>

    ΔA = A_j − A_i        ΔB = ((B_j − B_i + 90°) mod 180°) − 90°        S = |ΔA| / 6(t_j − t_i)  [rpm]

The modular reduction of \(\Delta B\) is the incremental counterpart of the ±90° wrap: it takes the shorter of the two equivalent yaw moves, so the wrap never becomes a 180° command.

The comparison with the original approach is the part I find most interesting. There the spindle speed was obtained from the winding angle at each block through \(S=\frac{F}{Y-\mathrm{d}y}\tan\alpha\cdot\frac{60}{2\pi}\), with the feed \(F\) chosen by trial between 0.2 and 1 in/s — the angle was imposed through the axis speeds. That works, but it couples geometry to timing: changing the feed changes the deposited angle. Here the geometry is settled first and the timing is a separate, subsequent optimisation, so the feed is free to be as fast as the machine allows without disturbing \(\alpha\). The two formulations agree when the eye lead is zero and the timing is uniform.
